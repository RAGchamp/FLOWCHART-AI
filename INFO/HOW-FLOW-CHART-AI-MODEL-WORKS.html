How the Flow Chart AI Model Works

When you click Trace it, a program called FlowTrace reads your picture and draws it as editable ribbons, labels, icons and badges. This page explains, step by step, what FlowTrace does with a picture. It then explains in plain English how its model learns to trace flow charts, how it gets better, why training takes a few nights on this computer, what happens during those nights, and how to watch the progress.

1. FlowTrace at a Glance

FlowTrace is the "brain" behind Trace it. It lives in the folder FLOW-CHART-APP\AI and runs on your own computer. Pictures never leave it. The app (App\index.html) and FlowTrace talk to each other through a small local service that you start with AI\start-flowtrace.bat.

The app only asks; FlowTrace does the seeing and reading; the app builds the shapes with its own tools.
Words used on this page
  • Model: a small program whose behaviour is learned from examples instead of written by hand. Here it's FlowTrace-Seg.
  • Classic vision: hand-written image rules FlowTrace uses until a model has been trained.
  • Training: showing the model thousands of pictures with their correct answers so it adjusts itself.
  • Answer key / pair: a picture together with its correct diagram (from Save training pair).
The three ways FlowTrace can answer
  1. Remembered answer: it has seen this exact picture (or slide) before and you corrected it, so it returns your answer. Perfect, but only for that picture.
  2. Trained model: once you have trained one, it marks where the ribbons, labels and center lines are, then exact geometry code builds the diagram.
  3. Classic vision: hand-written rules do the same marking. This is used now, and also as a safety net if a model gives odd results.

2. What Happens When You Click Trace it

Each step below is one file in AI\service. On the sample photo the whole run takes about 5–10 seconds (under 1 second when the answer is remembered).

The trace pipeline. Steps 3–8 are skipped when step 2 recognizes the picture.
STEP 1 — Straighten the picture
straighten.py→warp()→flatten_light()

The 4 orange corners you placed are used to "un-tilt" the photo into a flat rectangle about 1400 pixels wide, like a scanner. Uneven light (a bright spot, a dark corner, a grey screen) is evened out so the paper looks white everywhere.

The straightened picture is sent back to the app, which swaps it in behind your canvas so the traced shapes line up with it.

STEP 2 — Have I seen this picture before?
memory.py→find()→transform()

FlowTrace compares the picture with every answer key in AI\data\real. It doesn't compare pixel by pixel: it finds thousands of small distinctive spots (corners of letters, tips of arrows) and checks whether they line up with a saved picture. A match still works if you chose slightly different corners, or took a new photo of the same slide.

If at least 40 spots agree on one placement, your corrected diagram is moved onto the new picture and returned. You'll see "Recognized: your saved answer …". You can turn this off with Use remembered answers.

STEP 3 — Find the ribbons (this is where the model works)
segment.py→model() or classic()

For every pixel, FlowTrace decides: is this part of a ribbon? Is it a letter written on a ribbon? Is it on a ribbon's center line? It also measures how thick the ribbons are, and finds the numbered badges.

If a trained model exists (AI\models\flowtrace-seg.onnx), the model makes these decisions. Otherwise classic vision does: color thresholds, filling the holes left by letters, and dropping small blobs such as icons.

STEP 4 — Follow each ribbon along its middle, through crossings
graph.py→thin()→pair_ends()→walk_chains()

The ribbon area is shaved down to a one-pixel line along its middle. Where ribbons cross, this line forms an X. FlowTrace pairs the branches that continue each other most straightly (as a ribbon would), so it goes straight through the crossing instead of turning. Walking the line gives one continuous center line per ribbon. For the sample, that's the whole knot as one closed loop.

STEP 5 — Cut it into pieces, color them, decide which is on top
colors.py→find_gap_cuts()→color_cuts()→analyse()
  • Cuts: the thin white chevrons across a ribbon, and places where the color changes for good.
  • Flow direction: which way the chevrons point.
  • Piece colors: the typical color of each piece, leaving out the letters.
  • Over / under: at each crossing, the color in the middle belongs to the ribbon on top.
STEP 6 — Read the labels
labels.py→unroll()→read_strips()→fix_word()

Curved text is hard to read, so each piece is unrolled into a flat strip, like peeling a label off a bottle. Each strip is read four ways (both directions, two contrast settings) by Tesseract, a free text reader, in a single call. The best reading is snapped to a known process word from AI\datagen\words.txt when it's close ("TRAW" becomes TRAIN, "COLLE" becomes COLLECT).

Free text (title, names in the loops) and badge numbers are read the same way.

STEP 7 — Icons, badges, title
objects.py→loop_interiors()→find()

The inside of each loop is found. A name inside a loop (Data, ML, DEV, OPS) becomes an icon item; its icon is chosen from the name (Data → cloud, ML → network, DEV → code, OPS → gears). Circles with a number become badges. The biggest line near the top becomes the title, and the line under it becomes the subtitle.

STEP 8 — Editable bands (for "Stop after bands")
fit.py→fit_chain()

Each center line is also turned into the app's own Arc and Bézier bands: an arc where the line follows a circle, a Bézier curve elsewhere. These are used only when you tick Stop after bands, so you can reshape the curves before clicking Snap it.

STEP 9 — The app builds the shapes
pipeline.py→App: aiApply()

FlowTrace sends back a plan: center lines, cut points, piece colors and labels, icons, badges and title, each with a confidence. The app builds them with its own functions (the same ones as the Arrow tool and the panel) as one undo step, and lists anything with low confidence under Needs a look.

Takeaway: the model does only one job (step 3: "where are the ribbons, letters and center lines?"). Everything else is exact geometry code. That's why a very small model, one that can be trained on this computer, is enough.

3. The Model: FlowTrace-Seg

FlowTrace-Seg is a small neural network of a kind called a U-Net. It looks at the whole picture and paints three "highlighter" layers over it.

The U-Net first shrinks the picture to understand the big shapes (whole loops), then grows it back to mark exact pixels. Three output layers, one per question.
FactValueWhat it means
Sizeabout 250,000 numbers ("weights")Tiny: big image AIs have billions. Small enough to train on a laptop processor.
Input256 × 256 px (training), 384 × 384 (tracing)Pictures are shrunk to this size before the model looks at them.
Outputsribbon, text, centerThree layers with a 0–1 "how sure" value per pixel.
FilesAI\train\model_seg.py → AI\models\flowtrace-seg.onnxThe design; the trained, published model.
Safety netfalls back to classic visionIf the model's ribbons look very different from classic vision's (area or thickness), that picture uses classic vision.

Today there is no trained model yet. The panel says "classic vision". It switches to "trained model" after you train, run export_onnx.py, and restart FlowTrace.

4. How the Model Learns (Plain English)

Think of a student doing practice sheets that come with an answer key. The model starts out knowing nothing: its 250,000 numbers are random, and its first guesses are noise. Learning is the same small loop, repeated hundreds of thousands of times:

One "step" of training. The nudge is small on purpose: many small corrections add up to a model that works on pictures it has never seen.
  1. Show a picture. A practice diagram is made to look like a phone photo of a slide: tilted, blurred, with uneven light (section 5).
  2. The model guesses. It paints its three layers: ribbon, text, center line.
  3. Mark the guess. The guess is compared with the exact answer, pixel by pixel. The total "wrongness" is one number called the loss. Lower is better.
  4. Nudge. Mathematics (the "gradient") tells, for each of the 250,000 numbers, which way to move it to make the loss a little smaller. Every number is moved a tiny bit that way.

Pictures are shown 8 at a time (a batch). Going once through all the practice pictures is an epoch. After many epochs the model has learned what ribbons look like in general: their thickness, their smooth curves, white letters on color, and how one ribbon continues under another. That's why it can then trace new pictures, not just the practice ones.

Learning vs remembering

Remembered answers (step 2) store your corrected diagrams and give them back for that picture. Learning changes the model so it gets better at every picture. You need both: memory for diagrams you use again and again, learning for new ones.

The exam it never studies for

A small set of pictures (the validation set) is never used for nudging. After each epoch the model is tested on them. If it does well on pictures it never practised on, it has really learned, not just memorized the practice sheets.

6. How It Improves Over Time

The improvement cycle. Your corrections are the most valuable training data there is.
What training improves
  • Finding ribbons in messy photos: glare, angles, blur, 3-D shading, gradients.
  • Following ribbons through crossings (the center layer is trained on the hidden parts too).
  • Telling letters from ribbon, so pieces and colors come out cleaner.
  • Fewer false cuts at crossings.
What it does not improve (yet)
  • Which letters a label spells: that's Tesseract (step 6). Helped by sharper pictures and by adding your words to words.txt. A planned next step is to train a small label reader of our own.
  • Box-and-arrow flowcharts: the app has no box tools.
Measuring improvement: score.py

Pairs in AI\data\real\test are the frozen test set. They are never trained on, so they give an honest score. Run python score.py before and after each training. It prints a table and writes AI\eval\report.html with side-by-side pictures.

7. Why Training Takes a Few Nights

Training is millions of small multiplications repeated hundreds of thousands of times. Big AI labs do this on graphics cards (GPUs) that run thousands of multiplications at once. This computer has no such graphics card: a 4-core Intel Pentium Silver N5030 processor does all the work. It was measured on 2026-10-18:

ItemValue
Time per practice picture (guess + mark + nudge), 256 px≈ 0.4 s
Practice pictures20,000
Epochs needed for a good first model≈ 10
20,000 × 10 × 0.4 s≈ 22 hours (19–23 h)
So, with 8 hours a nightabout 3 nights
Fine-tuning later on new real pairs1–2 hours
Why so many repetitions?

Each nudge is deliberately tiny. Big nudges would make the model chase the last picture it saw and forget the others. Many small nudges over many different pictures make it learn what all ribbons have in common. 10 epochs means every practice picture is seen about 10 times, each time with a different photo look.

Why at night, and why that's safe

Training keeps all 4 processor cores busy, so the computer is slow for other work. At night nobody notices. It resumes where it stopped: --hours 8 stops after 8 hours, and the next night simply continues. Ctrl+C, a crash or a restart lose at most 10 minutes. A free cloud GPU (Kaggle, Colab) could do it in about an hour; that's allowed for the synthetic pictures only.

8. What Happens During the Night

A night of training, with the steps it repeats, its safety saves and its exams.
NIGHT 1 — Start

It loads the practice pictures, builds the model (random at first), and writes "training seg-v1: 20000 pictures per epoch (2500 steps of 8) …". If runs\seg-v1\last.pt exists, it says "resuming: epoch 3, step 7500 …" instead and continues.

EVERY STEP — about 3 seconds

8 practice pictures get a fresh photo look, the model guesses, the guesses are marked (loss), and every weight is nudged. Every 20 steps (about a minute) one progress line is printed and added to the log.

EVERY 10 MINUTES — safety save

Everything is saved to runs\seg-v1\last.pt: the model, the optimizer and the progress counters. If the computer sleeps, restarts or you press Ctrl+C, at most 10 minutes are lost.

EVERY EPOCH — about 2–2.5 hours: the exam

The model is tested on the 1,000 validation pictures it never practises on. It prints "validation IoU {ribbon, text, center} score …". If the score is its best so far, the model is copied to runs\seg-v1\best.pt: that's the one you publish later.

MORNING — stop, look, maybe publish

After --hours it stops by itself: "stopped; saved … Best validation score 0.xxx". Look at the log (section 9). When the best score is 0.6 or more, publish and test:

python export_onnx.py → restart FlowTrace → python score.py. If the score is still lower, export refuses to publish, and the app keeps using classic vision.

Before you leave it overnight
  • Plug in the charger. In Windows Settings → Power, set "Sleep" to Never while plugged in. A sleeping laptop pauses training (it resumes when it wakes up).
  • Close FlowTrace and other heavy programs. Training wants all 4 cores.
  • Expect the fan to run and the laptop to get warm. That's normal.
